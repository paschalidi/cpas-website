# We hired two engineers. Then we fired four.

I want to tell this story without the usual startup post-mortem polish. No "lessons learned." No "grateful for the experience." Just what happened.

## The hiring

We were building AI agents for a marketplace. The system searched a vector database, planned interviews, and sent messages. It worked in demos. It did not have users.

The C-suite told us to hire. Across the company, not just engineering. They needed headcount. They needed a team photo for the pitch deck. They needed to show investors that this was a real company, not two engineers and a PowerPoint.

We hired two engineers. Good people. One was great at TypeScript, had worked with LLMs before. The other understood data pipelines, had built vector search systems. We needed both.

They joined in January. By February we had five people in engineering. By March we had four.

## The lie

In the all-hands in March, the CEO said we had four months of runway. The team went quiet. Four months. We had been hiring people, telling them we were building something stable, and the bank account said four months.

I found out later that the real number was probably lower. The CEO had been shopping the company to investors since December. No traction. No term sheets. The existing investors had stopped returning calls. The demo that was supposed to close the Series A had not impressed anyone.

The hiring was not for the product. It was for the pitch. Hire fast, look like a real company, raise money before the cash runs out. The plan failed. So the people became a line item to cut.

## The firing

They asked us to tell everyone. Not HR. Not the CEO. The engineering leads. They gave us a script and a time: Thursday afternoon.

I sat in a room with two people I had recruited. One had a two-year-old. The other had just signed a lease. I told them the company was sunsetting the project. That their roles no longer existed. That this was not about performance. That they were good engineers and this was not their fault.

It was true and it was not enough.

The other two layoffs were existing team members. One had been there since the founding team. He had stock options that were now worth less than the paper they were printed on. He had a family. He asked me if he should have seen this coming. I said no. I was lying.

We all should have seen it coming.

## The signs we ignored

The C-suite demoed the AI agent to investors in February. They came back excited. "Great meeting. They loved the vision." No term sheet. No follow-up. Just "great meeting."

We had three beta users. All of them were friends of the founders. None of them paid. The metrics deck claimed "active marketplace engagement." It was three people clicking around a prototype.

The hiring requests kept coming. "We need a designer. We need a PM. We need more engineers." The product was not ready for a designer. There was no product to manage. But the org chart needed to look full.

I told myself this was startup life. Everyone stretches the truth. Everyone believes the vision will work out. I was wrong. There is a difference between optimism and theater. This was theater.

## What I did next

I stayed until the end because I had promised the two engineers I hired that I would not leave them. I helped them find jobs. One went to a Series B company doing actual AI infrastructure. The other went freelance. Both are doing better now.

The existing team members landed on their feet too. The market in 2025 is weird. Everyone is hiring and firing at the same time.

I did not write a LinkedIn post about it. I did not "announce my next chapter." I took a month off. I thought about whether I wanted to work at a startup again.

## The real lesson

I do not have a pithy takeaway. The lesson is not "do more due diligence on the runway" — though you should. The lesson is not "don't trust C-suite optimism" — though you shouldn't.

The lesson is that when a company hires people to impress investors rather than to build a product, the people are the first thing sacrificed when the theater ends. They are not employees. They are props. And when the show is over, the props get packed away.

If you are in a startup and the hiring feels faster than the product, trust that feeling. If the all-hands is full of "great meetings" and no numbers, trust that feeling. If you are asked to hire someone for a role that does not need to exist yet, ask why.

You might not like the answer.
